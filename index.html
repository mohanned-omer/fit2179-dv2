Australia's Digital Workforce: Boom, Bust & Beyond

From 14,000 tech workers in 1984 to over 380,000 at peak, Australia built one of the world's fastest-growing digital workforces — then watched it correct sharply after 2022. This is the full story: the rise, the crash, and what a fractured, suburb-by-suburb digital economy looks like today.

Act 1 · The big picture

Forty years of building a tech nation

Australia's tech workforce was once a niche — a few thousand specialists running mainframes and writing code for banks. By 2022 it had become one of the country's largest professional classes, with over 380,000 people employed in computer system design alone. Then the market corrected. Here is the full arc.

Act 2 · The demand shock

The 2022 hiring wave and what came after

When the hiring wave peaked in 2022, it was software developers and systems analysts doing the heaviest lifting. When it broke, they fell the furthest. But not every role was equal — and the gaps reveal which skills the market still genuinely needs.

Act 3 · Where the jobs are

Sydney and Melbourne dominate — but for how long?

Look at any national tech jobs map and one thing is immediately obvious: Australia's digital workforce is an eastern seaboard story, with NSW and Victoria anchoring roughly two-thirds of the country's employment. But the balance is shifting — slowly, and not always in the direction you'd expect.

NSW + Victoria hold 60.8% of national ICT vacancies at March 2026 — or 4,727 of 7,778 state-level job ads. For a Perth or Brisbane tech worker, the advertised market is roughly one-fifth the size of Sydney's.

Act 4 · Inside the local ecosystem

Not one digital economy — seven

Zoom down to individual suburbs — called SA2 regions — and Australia's digital economy splinters into seven distinct archetypes. A Bondi Beach tech precinct looks nothing like a Parramatta corporate hub, which looks nothing like a rural WA mining town with a handful of IT contractors. Understanding which type of place you live in explains your job prospects, your wage, and who your employer probably is.

Major hubs combine high job density with solid income premiums, while high-income specialists earn the largest wage premiums despite smaller local job bases. Consultant-heavy areas show the opposite: many solo businesses, very few employees. Use the filter below to highlight each type across both charts simultaneously.

Act 5 · The value of digital work

Digital work pays more — but not everywhere equally

Digital work pays more than average — that much is clear. But "more" covers a wide range. In some suburbs, working in tech means earning $39,000 above the local average wage. In others, the premium has evaporated entirely. The gap depends less on your skills than on where you work and what kind of digital ecosystem surrounds you.

Chart 8
Where you live is worth more than your job title
Top SA2 regions by income premium: how much more digital workers earn than the local average, minimum 500 digital jobs
How to read this chart

Each row is a suburb. The open circle shows the local average wage for all workers. The filled circle shows what digital workers in that suburb earn. The dollar label on the right is the gap — the premium that tech work commands in that location. Note: this measures premium above the local average, not the national average — a high premium in a lower-wage suburb may still mean lower absolute income than average Sydney wages.

Source: SA2 Digital Ecosystem Dataset, 2022-23
Act 6 · The business base

Most digital businesses are tiny

Behind every digital job is a business. But in Australia, the overwhelming majority of digital businesses are sole traders, micro-consultancies, and small firms with fewer than five employees. That structure shapes everything: how resilient the sector is, how income gets distributed, and why some ecosystems thrive while others stagnate.

Act 7 · The full picture

When local hiring froze, the visa tap stayed open

Vacancies, employment, business formation, and skilled migration move at different speeds. The full picture is not a single boom-or-bust story, but a labour market correction sitting on top of a broadening digital base — and a migration pipeline that quietly picked up the slack that local hiring left behind.

Chart 10
When local hiring froze, migration kept climbing
Skilled temporary resident visa grants by state and financial year — toggle between absolute count and state share
How to read this chart

Each coloured band is a state's contribution to total skilled visa grants that year. In "Absolute" mode, the total height of the chart shows national volume — watch it nearly double from 2021 to 2025. In "Share (%)" mode, each year sums to 100%, showing which states absorb the most skilled migrants. Important caveat: these grants cover all skilled occupations, not only tech roles — healthcare, engineering, and trades are also included.

Source: Department of Home Affairs BP0014 Temporary Resident (Skilled) Visas Granted

Key takeaways

  • The 2022 hiring surge corrected sharply, but the digital workforce did not disappear.
  • Digital Australia is highly uneven: state, suburb, and ecosystem type all shape opportunity.
  • The sector is broadening through small businesses, local ecosystems, and skilled migration rather than a single CBD jobs boom.

Demand cooled sharply

ICT vacancy ads are down -- from the 2022 peak — but employment has held up better than the headlines suggest.

Digital work is everywhere

-- Australian suburbs have a measurable digital workforce. Tech is no longer just a CBD story.

Seven kinds of digital place

-- ecosystem types shape what digital work looks like on the ground. Your postcode matters more than most job guides admit.

Australia's digital workforce is not shrinking — it is sorting. The post-2022 correction weeded out speculative hiring and left a leaner, more geographically concentrated demand. The businesses that remain are overwhelmingly small, the workers who thrive earn significant premiums, and the suburbs that lead are increasingly in the outer ring rather than the CBD. If you are entering the tech workforce in 2026, your location and local ecosystem type may matter more than your degree.
